How RAG Actually Works

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It's how AI assistants answer questions about your documents — documents they were never trained on. In the next few minutes you'll run a real RAG pipeline, step by step, on a document of your choice.

📄 Document→ ✂️ Chunks→ 🧭 Embeddings→ 🔍 Retrieval→ 💬 Answer
Step 1

Pick a document

Large language models (the AI behind chatbots) only know what was in their training data. They have never seen your meeting notes, your company wiki, or that PDF you got yesterday.

RAG fixes this by letting the model look things up in your documents before answering — like an open-book exam instead of a memory test.

Everything starts with a document. Use our sample, paste your own text, or upload a .txt / .md file.

Step 2

Split the document into chunks

We can't hand the model the whole document for every question — long documents are slow, expensive, and full of irrelevant text that distracts the model. So we cut the document into bite-sized pieces called chunks.

Later, instead of searching the whole document, we'll search chunks and hand the model only the few that matter.

Chunk size is a trade-off you'll feel for yourself in a moment:

  • Small chunks → precise, focused matches… but a chunk may cut an idea in half.
  • Big chunks → full context preserved… but each chunk mixes several topics, making matches fuzzier.
Step 3

Turn chunks into embeddings

Computers can't compare meaning directly — but they're great at comparing numbers. An embedding converts a piece of text into a list of numbers (a point in space) such that texts with similar meaning land close together.

Think of it as a map of meaning: "the queen lays eggs" and "the queen is the only fertile bee" end up as neighbors, while "stock prices fell" lands far away — even though none of them share many words.

Real systems use neural networks producing hundreds of dimensions. To keep things visible, this demo uses a simpler word-statistics embedding (TF-IDF) and squashes it down to 2D — the core idea, similar meaning ⇒ nearby points, is exactly the same.

👇 Each dot is one chunk of your document. Click a dot to inspect what its embedding "noticed".

Semantic space — distance ≈ difference in meaning. Axes have no units; only relative position matters.

Click a dot to inspect that chunk's embedding.

Step 4

Ask a question

Everything so far happened before any question — chunking and embedding are done once and stored in a vector database. Now the live part: a user shows up and asks something.

Step 5

Embed the question

The question goes through the same embedding process as the chunks, so it lands in the same semantic space. That's the trick that makes search possible: once the question is a point on the same map, "find relevant text" becomes "find the nearest dots".

Step 6

Retrieve the best chunks

Now we measure how close every chunk is to the question, using cosine similarity — a score from 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical direction in meaning-space). We keep the top-k highest-scoring chunks and ignore the rest.

This is the R in RAG: Retrieval.

Lines connect the question to its retrieved chunks. Thicker line = higher similarity.

Step 7

Build the prompt

The retrieved chunks get pasted — literally, as plain text — into a prompt for the language model. No magic: the model simply receives instructions, the chunks as "context", and the question.

Models charge and limit by tokens (word-pieces; roughly 4 characters ≈ 1 token). This is why we retrieve a few chunks instead of sending the whole document.
